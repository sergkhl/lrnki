# Critical Thinking

Critical thinking is the disciplined practice of deciding what to believe and what to do. It is not a habit of rejecting every claim, winning every argument, or treating emotion as an enemy. It is a way to make the structure of reasoning visible, test that reasoning against appropriate evidence, and adjust confidence when the situation changes.

Good critical thinking combines several abilities. A thinker must understand what is being claimed, distinguish reasons from conclusions, recognize what would follow if the reasons were true, judge whether the evidence is strong enough, and notice important alternatives. The final step is practical: choose or revise a belief without pretending to have more certainty than the evidence permits.

## 1. Claims, Reasons, Conclusions, and Arguments

A **claim** is a statement that can be accepted or rejected. “The meeting begins at nine,” “Regular practice usually improves performance,” and “This plan is too risky” are claims. Questions and commands can matter in a discussion, but they are not themselves claims unless they imply an answer. “Should we leave?” asks for a claim; “We should leave now” makes one.

An **argument** is a set of claims in which one or more claims are offered as support for another. The supporting claims are **reasons** or **premises**. The supported claim is the **conclusion**. In ordinary conversation, “argument” often means a quarrel. In reasoning, it means a support relationship, whether or not anyone is upset.

Consider:

> The northern road is closed because of flooding. The southern road remains open. Therefore, we should take the southern road.

The road conditions are reasons. The proposed choice is the conclusion. The word “therefore” signals the conclusion, while “because” often signals a reason. Other conclusion indicators include “so,” “thus,” and “which means that.” Other reason indicators include “since,” “given that,” and “as shown by.” These words are clues, not guarantees. Context determines the actual structure.

An argument can leave some reasoning unstated. If someone says, “The northern road is closed, so take the southern road,” the argument probably relies on an implicit premise such as “We need a usable road to reach our destination.” Reconstructing an unstated premise is useful when it makes the reasoning understandable without changing the speaker’s position. A charitable reconstruction uses the weakest plausible assumption needed to connect the stated reason to the conclusion. It does not invent an extreme or foolish version that is easy to attack.

Several claims near one another do not automatically form an argument. A report might explain a sequence of events without using one event to justify belief in another. An **explanation** usually starts with something treated as true and tells why or how it happened. An argument tries to establish that its conclusion is true or worth doing. “The alarm sounded because smoke reached the sensor” explains an accepted event. “The alarm probably sounded because smoke reached the sensor” may be an argument if the cause is still in question. The same sentence can play different roles depending on what the audience doubts.

Arguments also differ from mere repetition. Saying a conclusion again in stronger language does not add support. “This policy is unfair because it is plainly unjust” gives two close labels for the same judgment. A useful reason should be independently assessable and should increase support for the conclusion if it is true.

Before evaluating an argument, state it clearly:

1. Identify the main conclusion.
2. List the reasons offered for it.
3. Add only the implicit premises needed to make the connection intelligible.
4. Separate background information, examples, and emotional language from the support structure.
5. Clarify vague or shifting terms.

This reconstruction prevents a common mistake: judging a tone, identity, or isolated sentence instead of judging the actual reasoning.

## 2. Deduction: Validity and Soundness

A **deductive argument** presents its conclusion as necessary: if the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false. Deduction is about what follows from what, not about how confident or persuasive the speaker sounds.

An argument is **valid** when there is no possible case in which all its premises are true and its conclusion is false. Validity concerns structure. It does not say that the premises are actually true.

> Every item in the sealed box is metal.
>
> The key is an item in the sealed box.
>
> Therefore, the key is metal.

This form is valid. If both premises are true, the conclusion must be true. If the first premise is false because the box also contains paper, that would make the argument unreliable in the actual situation, but it would not change the validity of its form.

A deductive argument is **sound** when it is valid and all its premises are true. Soundness combines correct structure with accurate starting points. A sound argument guarantees a true conclusion. An unsound argument may have a false premise, an invalid form, or both.

Testing validity requires temporarily granting the premises. Ask: could those premises all be true while the conclusion is false? If a coherent counterexample is possible, the argument is invalid.

One familiar valid pattern is:

> If the archive is locked, then a key is required.
>
> The archive is locked.
>
> Therefore, a key is required.

The phrase “if A, then B” says that A is sufficient for B. It does not say that B occurs only when A occurs. Confusing these directions creates invalid reasoning:

> If the archive is locked, then a key is required.
>
> A key is required.
>
> Therefore, the archive is locked.

Perhaps the archive is unlocked but a separate cabinet requires a key. The premises can be true while the conclusion is false. This mistake treats a result as proof of one particular cause.

Another valid pattern is:

> If the archive is locked, then a key is required.
>
> A key is not required.
>
> Therefore, the archive is not locked.

By contrast, denying the first part does not justify denying the second:

> If the archive is locked, then a key is required.
>
> The archive is not locked.
>
> Therefore, a key is not required.

A key might still be required for another reason.

Words such as “all,” “some,” “none,” “only,” “unless,” and “must” strongly affect deductive structure. “Only registered members may enter” means that being registered is necessary for entry; it does not mean every registered member will enter. “All copper conducts electricity” does not imply that everything conducting electricity is copper. Reversing a classification rule is a common error.

Definitions can support deduction, but only if the same meaning is maintained. If “light” first means low in weight and later means visible radiation, a conclusion may appear to follow only because the term has shifted. This is **equivocation**. Precise definitions matter most when a dispute turns on category boundaries.

Deduction is especially useful for rules, classifications, mathematics, contracts, and consistency checks. It cannot rescue poor premises. A flawless deduction from inaccurate assumptions remains unsound, so checking validity and checking premise truth are separate tasks.

## 3. Induction, Evidence, and Uncertainty

Most everyday reasoning is not deductive. We predict weather, infer why a machine failed, estimate whether a treatment helps, and generalize from samples. These are **inductive** inferences: the premises make a conclusion more or less probable but do not guarantee it.

An inductive argument is **strong** when its evidence gives substantial support to its conclusion. It is **weak** when the same evidence is compatible with too many plausible alternatives. Strength comes in degrees and depends on background information.

Suppose 800 randomly selected commuters in a city are surveyed, and 62 percent report using public transport at least once a week. This may support a careful conclusion about commuters in that city during that period. It does not automatically support the same percentage for every resident, another city, or a future year. The quality of the inference depends on how people were selected, who did not respond, how the question was worded, and how closely the target population matches the sample.

Sample size matters, but representativeness matters too. A very large biased sample can give a precise estimate of the wrong population. An online poll open to volunteers may collect thousands of responses while overrepresenting people with strong views. Random selection can reduce selection bias because each eligible member has a known chance of inclusion, but practical failures such as nonresponse can still distort results.

Individual examples are useful for showing that something can happen. One counterexample can refute a universal claim such as “This device never fails in cold weather.” A few examples usually cannot establish a population rate or a general causal rule. **Anecdotes** are memorable cases; their vividness does not tell us how common or representative they are.

Evidence updates a prior level of plausibility. If a rare condition and a common condition can both produce the same symptom, observing the symptom does not make the two causes equally likely. Their initial frequency, the reliability of the observation, and the presence of other evidence all matter. A highly accurate test can still produce a meaningful share of false alarms when the tested condition is extremely rare.

The absence of expected evidence can also matter. If a hypothesis strongly predicts a visible trace and careful inspection finds none, confidence in the hypothesis should fall. But “no evidence found” is not always “evidence of absence.” Failure to find a faint trace with a weak detector says less than failure to find a large trace with a sensitive detector. The question is whether the search had a good chance of finding the evidence if it existed.

Competing explanations should be compared by how well they account for the full pattern, not merely whether each can be made compatible with one fact. Useful considerations include:

- **Fit:** Does the explanation account for the important observations?
- **Predictive success:** Did it correctly anticipate evidence not used to invent it?
- **Simplicity:** Does it avoid unnecessary assumptions while preserving explanatory power?
- **Coherence:** Is it consistent with well-supported background knowledge?
- **Robustness:** Does it survive reasonable changes in measurement or analysis?
- **Specificity:** Does it rule out outcomes that rival explanations would allow?

Simplicity does not mean “the shortest story must be true.” Reality can be complicated. Simplicity is a preference against extra assumptions that do no explanatory work.

Uncertainty should appear in the conclusion. Evidence may support “often,” “probably,” “under these conditions,” or “the best current explanation” rather than “always” or “proved.” A conclusion narrower than the evidence is usually safer than one that reaches beyond the studied population, setting, outcome, or time period.

## 4. Judging Sources and Evidence Quality

A source is a route through which information reaches us: a direct observation, dataset, report, witness, expert analysis, institution, or summary. Source evaluation is not a search for labels that make acceptance automatic. A reputable source can make a mistake, and an unfamiliar source can report something true. The aim is to estimate reliability for this particular claim.

Start with **proximity to the evidence**. A primary record may contain original measurements, statements, or documents. A secondary source interprets or summarizes primary material. Primary does not always mean better: raw records may be hard to interpret, while a careful synthesis may place many studies in context. But knowing the distance helps reveal where selection and interpretation entered.

Then examine **relevant competence**. Expertise is specific. Skill in one field does not grant authority in every neighboring field. Relevant indicators include demonstrated training, methods that peers can inspect, a history of accurate work, and engagement with evidence rather than status alone. Expert agreement is especially informative when experts use different methods yet converge, though consensus remains open to revision.

Consider **incentives and independence**. Financial, political, social, or reputational interests can influence what is studied, reported, or omitted. A conflict of interest is a reason for scrutiny, not proof of falsehood. Transparent disclosure allows readers to weigh it. Evidence replicated by groups with different incentives is often more reassuring than a single interested report.

Inspect the **method**. Ask what was measured, how cases were selected, what comparison was used, how uncertainty was represented, and whether the analysis was chosen before or after seeing results. Measurement validity matters: a convenient indicator may not capture the intended concept. If “engagement” is measured only by clicks, the measure may miss sustained attention and reward accidental taps.

Look for **corroboration**. Independent sources that rely on genuinely separate evidence provide more support than many sites repeating one original statement. Ten summaries copied from the same press release are not ten independent confirmations. Trace a claim backward when possible.

Check **scope and date**. An accurate historical statistic may no longer describe the present. Evidence from one setting may not transfer to another. A headline often compresses a conditional finding into a universal claim. Reading enough context to recover the population, intervention or exposure, comparison, outcome, and time frame prevents many errors.

Distinguish the source’s evidence from its **interpretation**. A table of observations may be reliable even when a commentary draws an exaggerated conclusion. Conversely, a plausible explanation cannot compensate for invented or selectively reported data.

Warning signs include anonymous factual assertions where accountability is feasible, missing methods, inaccessible supporting material, altered images, internally inconsistent numbers, emotionally loaded certainty, and refusal to acknowledge corrections. None is an infallible test. Reliable judgment comes from several checks pointing in the same direction.

When a claim matters, use **lateral reading**: leave the page and see how independent knowledgeable sources describe the publisher, evidence, and dispute. This is often more efficient than studying the visual polish of a single page. Professional design can accompany either sound or unsound content.

## 5. Causal Reasoning

A causal claim says that changing one factor helps produce a change in another. Causal reasoning is harder than noticing that two things vary together.

A **correlation** or association means that two variables are related in observed data. It does not by itself establish which variable causes the other, whether a third factor affects both, or whether the pattern is accidental.

Imagine that neighborhoods with more emergency vehicles at a fire tend to have more property damage. Sending fewer vehicles would not reduce the damage. Fire severity causes both the number of vehicles and the damage. Severity is a **confounder**: a factor related to both the proposed cause and the outcome that can create or distort an association.

Direction can also be unclear. Stress may reduce sleep, while poor sleep may increase stress. A one-way story may miss a feedback loop. Sometimes selection creates an apparent relationship. If a study includes only people who have already passed a demanding test, relationships among preparation, experience, and performance inside that selected group may differ from those in the full population.

A good causal comparison asks what would have happened to the same units without the proposed cause. That alternative cannot usually be observed at the same time, so research approximates it with a **comparison group**. Random assignment, when feasible and ethical, helps balance both known and unknown background factors across groups. The groups can then differ mainly in the assigned intervention.

Random assignment and random sampling solve different problems. Random assignment supports causal comparison within a study. Random sampling supports generalization from a sample to a population. A study can have one without the other.

Experiments are not always possible. Observational studies can strengthen causal inference through careful timing, matched comparisons, natural changes, repeated measurements, and adjustment for measured confounders. These methods rely on assumptions and usually require more caution about hidden differences.

Several kinds of evidence together make a causal claim stronger:

- The proposed cause occurs before the outcome.
- Changes in exposure are followed by appropriately patterned changes in outcome.
- Plausible alternatives have been tested or controlled.
- A credible mechanism connects cause and effect.
- Results recur across settings, measurements, or methods.
- Intervening on the cause changes the outcome as predicted.

No single item is always decisive. A mechanism without observed effect is not enough, and repeated association without temporal order cannot establish the direction.

Causal claims also need a defined contrast. “Exercise improves health” is underspecified: what kind and amount of exercise, compared with what alternative, for which people, over what period, and which health outcome? Precise contrasts reveal that a causal effect can vary across conditions.

Avoid turning a population average into a guarantee for an individual. An intervention may improve an outcome on average while having little effect, a larger effect, or an adverse effect for particular people. Average effects remain useful, but their scope must be stated.

## 6. Common Reasoning Failures

Reasoning failures are recurring patterns that can make a conclusion look better supported than it is. Naming a pattern is only the beginning. A label does not refute an argument; the task is to explain exactly where support breaks down.

A **straw person** replaces a position with a weaker or more extreme version, then attacks the substitute. Fair evaluation uses the strongest reasonable interpretation supported by what was actually said. Charity has limits: it clarifies ambiguity but does not hide a genuine defect.

An **ad hominem** response attacks a person instead of addressing the argument. Personal information can be relevant when credibility is itself evidence, as with a witness’s access or a disclosed conflict. It is irrelevant when it merely distracts from independently checkable reasons.

A **false dilemma** presents two options as if they were exhaustive when other possibilities or combinations exist. Some choices really are binary, so the error is not the use of two options but the unjustified exclusion of alternatives.

**Circular reasoning** assumes the conclusion in its support. “This rule is correct because it is the right rule” adds no independent reason. Larger circles can be less obvious when several claims ultimately depend on one another without external support.

A **hasty generalization** draws a broad conclusion from too few or poorly selected cases. The remedy is not always more examples; it is a sample and method suited to the target claim.

An **appeal to popularity** treats widespread belief as proof. Popularity may be evidence about preferences, conventions, or what people believe, but not necessarily about an external fact. Conversely, rejecting a claim merely because it is popular is no better.

An **appeal to authority** relies on status without establishing relevant expertise, accurate representation, or appropriate agreement. Responsible reliance on specialists is unavoidable in complex societies. The mistake lies in substituting prestige for relevant evidence or treating one expert as conclusive when qualified experts substantially disagree.

**Moving the goalposts** changes the standard of proof after the original standard has been met. Standards can legitimately change when new stakes or defects emerge, but the reason for the change should be stated and applied consistently.

**Cherry-picking** selects favorable observations while ignoring relevant contrary evidence. A related practice is searching many analyses and reporting only the one that supports a desired result. Predeclared measures, complete reporting, and replication reduce this risk.

The **base-rate neglect** error ignores how common an event was before new evidence arrived. A dramatic clue can dominate attention even when it occurs frequently under ordinary alternatives.

The **sunk-cost error** treats unrecoverable past effort or expense as a reason to continue. Decisions should compare the future costs and benefits of available options. Past investment can matter indirectly—for example, because it created useful skills—but its mere irrecoverability is not a future benefit.

Motivated reasoning is broader than any named fallacy. People naturally inspect unwelcome evidence more critically than welcome evidence. A useful defense is to ask what evidence would change one’s mind, seek the strongest opposing case, and apply the same standards to favored and disfavored conclusions.

## 7. Calibrated Revision and Better Decisions

Critical thinking ends neither in permanent doubt nor instant certainty. It aims for **calibration**: confidence that matches the quality and quantity of evidence.

Beliefs can be held at degrees such as possible, plausible, likely, very likely, or established within a defined scope. These terms are imperfect, but they are better than treating every issue as simply true or false before the evidence is mature. Where stakes justify it, numerical probability ranges can make disagreements clearer. Precision should not exceed what the evidence supports.

Updating should depend on how surprising the new evidence would be under competing views. Evidence predicted by many explanations changes little. Evidence strongly expected by one explanation and unlikely under rivals changes more. Repetition matters only when observations are sufficiently independent; hearing the same claim through five channels that copied one source should not multiply confidence fivefold.

Changing one’s mind is not a failure when the evidence changes. A responsible revision records what shifted: a premise proved false, a measurement improved, an alternative explanation gained support, or the costs of error changed. This creates continuity rather than arbitrary reversal.

Belief and action require different thresholds. A person may judge rain only moderately likely yet carry an umbrella because carrying it is cheap and getting soaked is costly. A decision depends on both probabilities and consequences.

A simple decision structure is:

1. Define the decision and the available options, including waiting or gathering information when feasible.
2. Identify outcomes that matter and the people affected.
3. Estimate how each option changes the likelihood of those outcomes.
4. Consider the size, reversibility, and distribution of benefits and harms.
5. Look for missing options, incentives, and second-order effects.
6. Choose a review point and evidence that would trigger revision.

Expected value is one useful idea: combine how good or bad an outcome would be with how likely it is. It does not capture every concern. Very large losses, fairness, rights, irreversibility, and uncertainty about the probabilities may require separate attention. A robust option performs acceptably across several plausible futures rather than brilliantly under only one forecast.

Information has value when it could change the choice enough to justify the cost and delay of obtaining it. More research is not automatically better. If all plausible findings lead to the same action, additional information has little decision value. If a reversible pilot can cheaply reveal an important uncertainty, testing may be wiser than extended debate.

Disagreement can reveal different premises, evidence, definitions, or values. Restating the other view in terms its holder recognizes often shows where the real divergence lies. Some disagreements are factual and can be investigated. Others involve priorities that evidence alone cannot settle, though evidence can still clarify consequences and tradeoffs.

The habits of critical thinking reinforce one another: reconstruct the argument fairly, distinguish deductive necessity from inductive support, inspect evidence at the right level, test causal alternatives, notice recurrent failures, and revise confidence in proportion to what has been learned. The goal is not immunity from error. It is a reliable way to find errors earlier, limit their consequences, and improve the next judgment.
